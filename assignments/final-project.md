# Final Project — Intelligence Analysis Ontology

The Final Project is a substantial ontology development project focused on intelligence analysis.

The project should demonstrate the ability to apply ontology methods to an intelligence-analysis problem, domain, or workflow.

The emphasis is on producing an ontology that is:

- coherent;
- appropriately scoped;
- useful for a clearly identified intelligence-analysis purpose;
- well documented; and
- supported by defensible modeling decisions.

A small ontology that is carefully designed, clearly documented, and well justified is preferable to a large ontology assembled without sufficient attention to modeling quality.

## Topic Requirement

The project must directly concern intelligence analysis.

Possible areas include:

- actors, organizations, events, capabilities, locations, threats, intentions, and relationships;
- structured analytic techniques;
- evidence, hypotheses, assumptions, indicators, or competing explanations;
- intelligence requirements and information needs;
- uncertainty, confidence, provenance, and source reliability;
- geopolitical, military, security, cyber, or economic intelligence;
- intelligence reporting and analytic products;
- semantic interoperability across intelligence datasets or systems;
- AI-generated ontologies for intelligence use cases; or
- ontology content used to ground, constrain, or evaluate AI-supported intelligence workflows.

Use only public, fictional, synthetic, or otherwise authorized information.

## Final Project Packet

### 1. Ontology Artifact

Submit the completed ontology in an appropriate machine-readable format, such as `.owl`, `.ttl`, or another appropriate RDF/OWL serialization.

### 2. Intelligence-Analysis Problem Statement

Provide a concise explanation of the intelligence domain, analytic problem, or workflow being addressed and why ontology is useful for that problem.

### 3. Scope Statement

Describe what the ontology represents and what it deliberately does not attempt to represent.

### 4. Competency Questions or Intended Analytic Uses

Provide a set of questions, tasks, or use cases that the ontology is intended to support.

Make clear how these relate to intelligence analysis.

### 5. Modeling Documentation

Explain the most important:

- classes;
- relations;
- definitions;
- axioms; and
- modeling decisions.

Explain **why** important design choices were made rather than merely listing ontology content.

Where relevant, include diagrams or small ontology examples.

### 6. Sources and Reused Content

Cite relevant scholarly literature, intelligence-analysis resources, standards, datasets, existing ontologies, and other materials.

Clearly identify any ontology content that has been reused, imported, adapted, or aligned.

### 7. Analytic Value and Limitations

Explain:

- how the ontology could support intelligence analysis;
- what limitations remain;
- what assumptions constrain its use; and
- what additional development would be necessary for a more mature or operational application.

## Evaluation and Validation

Evaluation and validation are part of the project grade.

Use methods appropriate to your project. These may include:

- checking whether competency questions can be answered;
- running an OWL reasoner;
- checking ontology consistency;
- inspecting inferred classifications;
- creating representative individuals or test cases;
- comparing the ontology against source material;
- checking whether definitions and axioms support the intended distinctions; or
- documenting known cases the ontology cannot yet represent.

You do not need to use every method above. Use methods that genuinely test the claims you make about your ontology.

## Graduate-Level Expectations

Graduate students complete the same core project but are expected to demonstrate greater conceptual and technical depth.

Strong graduate work will normally demonstrate several of the following:

- substantial reuse or alignment with existing ontology content;
- deeper engagement with relevant scholarly literature;
- explicit and well-justified formal axioms;
- automated reasoning where appropriate;
- systematic evaluation against competency questions;
- comparison of plausible alternative modeling approaches;
- a reusable design pattern;
- careful treatment of difficult conceptual distinctions; or
- documentation suitable for contribution to an open-source ontology project.

The distinction is one of **rigor, justification, evaluation, and depth**, not simply ontology size.

## Grading

Final projects are evaluated on:

- quality and viability of the ontology;
- relevance to intelligence analysis;
- appropriate application of course concepts and methods;
- coherence of the modeling;
- justification of important design decisions;
- quality of evaluation and validation;
- completeness of documentation;
- clarity;
- appropriate use of sources; and
- creativity.
